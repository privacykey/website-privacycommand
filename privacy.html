Privacy policy

Last updated: 30 April 2026

privacycommand is a privacy tool. It behaves like one. This page documents what the app does and does not do with your data, every outbound network call it can make, and how to take it fully offline if you'd rather.

The short version

  • No analytics. privacycommand ships zero analytics, advertising, or attribution SDKs. There is no telemetry endpoint, install counter, or crash-report bucket.
  • All analysis is local. The inspected app's contents never leave your machine.
  • Outbound calls are explicit and bounded. See the table below for every endpoint the app may contact.
  • The privileged helper is opt-in. Without it, the app still works, but file-event monitoring is unavailable and the network kill switch is disabled.
  • Open source under MIT. Read the code, fork it, run it offline.

What we collect

Nothing. privacycommand has no server. There is no account to create, email to register, or licence key to validate. The maintainers have no way to know that you've installed the app, let alone what you've used it to inspect.

Every outbound endpoint the app may contact

privacycommand makes three categories of network call. Each one is bounded, can be switched off, and is documented here.

What we explicitly do not do

  • No analytics service: not Firebase, Mixpanel, Amplitude, Sentry, PostHog, Segment, or a homebrewed pixel.
  • No crash-report SDK. macOS' built-in crash reporter applies; it sends to Apple, not to us.
  • No referrer tracking when you click links from the app.
  • No fingerprinting. We don't know your machine's model, OS version, or chip family.

The privileged helper

privacycommand bundles an optional helper (org.privacykey.privacycommand.HelperTool) that runs as root. You're prompted to install it the first time you ask for a feature that needs it; you can decline, and the rest of the app keeps working. The helper exists to:

  • Run fs_usage on your behalf to capture file-event traces from the inspected app.
  • Run sfltool to enumerate background tasks the app has registered (so the audit doesn't trigger a system admin prompt).
  • Install and remove a pf packet-filter anchor when you flip the network kill switch on or off.

The helper validates clients by Team ID on connect, and a binary not signed by the same Team ID can't talk to it. The full helper source is in privacycommand/Sources/privacycommandHelper/ in the public repo.

Reports and what's in them

Every audit you run produces a report containing the inspected app's bundle ID, version, code-signing identity, and the output of every detector. Reports are written to ~/Library/Application Support/privacycommand/runs/ on your machine. They are never uploaded anywhere. You can export a report as JSON, HTML, or PDF; what you do with the export afterwards is up to you.

Going fully offline

You can run privacycommand with no outbound network at all by turning off three things in Settings:

  • App Store cross-check. Disable to skip itunes.apple.com and apps.apple.com requests.
  • Reverse DNS labels. Disable to skip system resolver queries during monitoring, after which destinations show as raw IPs.
  • Update checks. Off by default; verify under Settings → Updates that auto-checks are still off.

If you're on a managed network that blocks GitHub or Apple endpoints by policy, the app degrades gracefully. Features that require those endpoints are unavailable and the rest still works.
